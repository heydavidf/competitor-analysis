# Analysis method

Use this reference while running the analysis. It keeps the work on the customer's task and the proposed value.

This is the skill's own working method. Jaime Levy's *UX Strategy* teaches a related approach. This note does not quote or summarize that book.

## Intended Outcome

Competitive research collects market and UX evidence. Competitive analysis turns that evidence into intelligence and a recommendation about the viability and direction of an initial value proposition.

The analysis should help a product team understand current solutions, experience relevant customer journeys, recognize competitive parity, find repeated friction and gaps, identify a differentiated experience or business model worth validating, and take a defensible position even when it challenges the initial idea.

It is not a race to collect the most features, company facts, or frameworks.

## Required Framing

Start with four linked statements:

1. **Target customer:** the specific segment whose choice is being studied.
2. **Problem or desired outcome:** what that customer needs to accomplish.
3. **Initial value proposition:** the promised value and proposed solution.
4. **Critical task:** the customer journey that best demonstrates delivery of that value.

If any statement changes during research, update the competitor classification and explain the change. A product may move between direct and indirect as the value proposition becomes clearer.

## Competitor Meaning

- **Direct competitor:** offers the same or a substantially similar value proposition to the same target customer. The implementation or business model may differ.
- **Indirect competitor:** offers a different value proposition or solution but satisfies all or part of the same customer need. This includes broader horizontal products, adjacent categories, manual workarounds, tool combinations, and do-nothing behavior when they are genuine substitutes.

The classification question is not "does this company look like us?" It is "could the target customer choose this instead to accomplish the task?"

## Discovery Sequence

1. Enter the target customer's headspace.
2. Generate the words and phrases they would use to solve the problem.
3. Expand them through predictive suggestions, related searches, reviews, forums, stakeholder input, customer interviews, adjacent markets, regions, and languages.
4. Keep a broad longlist before pruning.
5. Visit each candidate's primary experience and decide whether its value proposition genuinely competes.
6. Preserve a diverse mix of direct and indirect alternatives.
7. Group comparable competitors by value proposition, business model, platform, market type, or another evidence-based similarity.

Search rankings and comparison lists are leads, not proof of market leadership.

## Research Matrix Logic

Rows are competitors. Columns are attributes that allow relevant cross-comparison. Adapt the columns to the value proposition instead of treating every possible attribute as mandatory.

### Core Attributes

- Competitor and primary URL.
- Direct/indirect type and logical subgroup.
- Relevant customer segment and value proposition.
- Why the product competes for the critical task.
- Revenue or business model.
- Critical-task experience and heuristic grade.
- Key differentiators or competitive advantages.
- Customer evidence.
- Competitor-perspective SWOT.
- Sources and evidence quality.

### Conditional Attributes

- Year founded and funding.
- Traffic, downloads, users, listings, transactions, or category-specific adoption measures.
- Product categories and content types.
- Personalization, UGC, crowdsourced data, and community.
- Social activity, customer reviews, and recent news.
- Pricing, integrations, security, regulation, or other category-specific factors.

Scale metrics are useful only when they help explain market position or threat. An indirect competitor's corporate funding or total traffic may be irrelevant when only one small part of its offering competes.

## Critical-Task UX Benchmark

Evaluate the smallest end-to-end journey that proves the value proposition. For every competitor, record:

- Entry point and platform.
- Preconditions, including account, payment, or sales contact.
- Major steps and outcome.
- Friction, dead ends, uncertainty, or effort.
- Particularly effective interaction patterns.
- Information, content, trust, or personalization that affects the task.
- An overall `A` to `F` heuristic grade with a concise rationale.
- Screenshots or stable page references when accessible and useful.

Use the same task and evaluation criteria across comparable competitors. Do not infer a logged-in experience from marketing copy. Mark untested flows as `Not observed`.

The benchmark seeks:

- **Parity:** the minimum experience customers already expect.
- **Pattern:** conventions or business models repeated across the market.
- **Gap:** an unmet need, repeated failure, or underserved segment.
- **Inspiration:** a strong interaction or business-model element that could inform a new combination.

Avoid "competitors have it, therefore we need it." A feature matters only if it helps the target customer accomplish the primary goal or supports a viable business model.

## Evidence Interpretation

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Observed | Directly seen in the product or a primary record |
| Claimed | Asserted by the competitor but not independently confirmed |
| Reported | Described by a customer or independent third party |
| Inferred | Analyst conclusion drawn from cited evidence |

Confidence describes support, not persuasiveness:

- **High:** direct primary evidence or consistent independent evidence.
- **Medium:** credible but incomplete, dated, or partly indirect evidence.
- **Low:** thin, conflicting, snippet-only, or heavily inferred evidence.

Customer reviews reveal possible patterns, not population statistics. Separate repeated themes from isolated anecdotes and try to reproduce important complaints in the product.

## Four-Step Analysis

### 1. Scan and check completeness

Review the matrix as a whole. Identify missing load-bearing attributes, uneven research depth, obvious competitors omitted, and facts that became stale or conflict across sources.

### 2. Create logical comparison groups

Compare like with like inside direct and indirect categories. Group by the attribute that best explains how the customer chooses.

### 3. Benchmark and SWOT

Compare attributes column by column. Identify high and low performers, market baselines, UX patterns, business-model differences, and serious threats.

Complete SWOT from the competitor's perspective:

- **Strengths:** what currently gives it an advantage.
- **Weaknesses:** what its target customer cannot do well or where delivery falls short.
- **Opportunities:** external or market opportunities it could exploit.
- **Threats:** external factors or competitors that could erode its position.

For indirect competitors, analyze only the relevant slice of the product.

### 4. Build the brief and take a stand

Build an evidence chain from the problem, customer, value proposition, date, and scope through the current marketplace, threatening competitors, detailed evidence, marketplace verdict, opportunity, and recommendation.

The recommendation may support the initial proposition, narrow it, change the target customer or problem, recommend a pivot, or call for more evidence.

## Marketplace Verdict

- **Red ocean:** the customer has many mature, similar choices and the proposed value is not yet meaningfully differentiated.
- **Purple ocean:** meaningful competition exists, but evidence points to a credible gap, underserved segment, or differentiated combination.
- **Blue ocean:** few direct alternatives exist and there is credible evidence of unmet need. Absence of competitors without demand evidence is uncertainty, not a blue ocean.

The recommendation must identify competitive parity, the shortcoming or unmet need to improve, the most important differentiated experience, the plausible business-model implication, and the smallest MVP, prototype, or concept test that can validate the hypothesis.

## Boundaries

- SWOT belongs as a view from each competitor's perspective.
- Business-model analysis belongs because a stronger experience still has to work as a business.
- Pricing, moat, and positioning maps are supplementary. They may clarify a decision but must not replace customer-task benchmarking.
- Market sizing, Porter's Five Forces, JTBD, category design, and other complete frameworks belong in other workflows unless the user explicitly requests them.
- Desk research can expose opportunity hypotheses. It cannot validate demand or product-market fit.
